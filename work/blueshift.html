Blueshift · Product design agency 2024–

Consulting with the price on the tag.

Blueshift is a product design consultancy in Minneapolis. Four of us started it in 2024: Nils Hansen, Heather Sowden, Kurt Schmidt, and me. We sell AI-enabled product design to software companies. The house line for what we sell against runs three sentences. Roadmaps drifted from business goals. Experiences became cluttered. Teams lost momentum.

The hard part of selling that is the shape of the sale. Consultancies quote projects, which means a discovery call, a proposal, and a number that lands a week later. A buyer who is only curious has nothing to compare, no way to start small, and every reason to keep shopping. Our own project work runs $30–80k and our retainers run $8–15k a month. Neither is a first step.

So we built three products instead and printed the prices. Spark, Pulse, and Surge. Fixed scope, fixed price, five days or less, each one live at its own address. The cheapest is $2,500.

Spark asks what. Pulse asks what's broken. Surge draws the answer.

Spark is the research one. Five days, AI synthesis of whatever research, analytics, and competitive material already exists, then a senior researcher on up to five customer calls to probe what the synthesis turned up. The buyer is a team with a business goal and no idea which feature moves it. One price, $9,500, and the fine print says participant incentives are extra.

Pulse is the audit. Analytics, session recordings, and feedback channels run through AI analysis to find drop-off and friction, then a senior designer does a structured heuristic pass on top so symptoms and root causes come apart. One screen is $2,500, one flow is $4,500, the whole product is $9,500. Pulse is the cheapest way into the studio.

Surge is the prototype. A live design jam with the client's team where requirements get sharpened on the spot and AI gets steered through several wireframe directions, then a craft pass that applies their style guide and annotates everything for developers. Only Surge hands over a Figma file. $4,950 for one screen, $9,500 for four, $19,500 for eight.

They stand alone and they stack. Each page names where the other two pick up, so the audit that finds the damage can point at the sprint that fixes it.

One page, three arguments.

All three pages are the same page. A two-line headline, one sentence saying what the thing is, a line naming who it's for, how it works, what you get, packages, questions, and a form. Read one and you have already learned how to read the other two, which is the point, since the three are meant to be read against each other and against a proposal that hasn't arrived yet.

The template bends where the product does. Spark runs four stages instead of three, because AI synthesis and human synthesis are separate steps and collapsing them into one would hide the part the client is paying a senior researcher for. Pulse ships three deliverables where the other two ship four. Surge is the only one whose deliverable grid leads with a Figma file, because it's the only one that produces one.

Everything else holds. No stage is called discovery. Every stage names something the client either does or receives. Nobody had to invent a new page to sell the next product, which is what makes a fourth one cheap.

Panels that run the work instead of describing it.

Every agency in the category says AI-enabled. That's a claim. A page of claims is how a buyer decides everyone is the same. I wrote up five directions for the section that introduces the three products and argued for the one I called the instrument panel: physical frames, monospace labels, blue for the data being processed and magenta for the signal the analysis found. The reason I gave was that it shows what the AI does rather than describing it.

Spark's runs a field of dark points across a ruled grid. A subset turns blue and pulls into a cluster while a monospace line under it counts up, "synthesizing multi-modal datastreams, in progress, 11%". That's the engagement: eighty scattered inputs, four themes.

Pulse's is a halftone field with a magenta core swelling through it, framed by instrument telemetry, "processing_nodes [ 56 ]" and "pattern_recognition: active", with a warning callout that fires mid-sweep. Surge's is quieter and physical: a plaster slab under a grid of real product screens, held on registration crosshairs, because the thing Surge produces is directions to choose between.

All three sit on the same photographed stone. Blue is the substrate, magenta is what got found, and there is no third color anywhere on the site to muddy that.

The hedge came out and the price doubled.

Surge shipped twice. The first one-pager priced it at $2,500, $5,000, and $10,000, and introduced it like this: "Surge is a time-boxed, collaborative design engagement, offered by Blueshift to help you validate features, rally stakeholders, and lock in estimates much earlier in the process. Not a replacement for comprehensive product design, but a valuable first step in the process."

The version that shipped reads "Surge blends advanced AI and senior designers so you can validate features, rally stakeholders, and lock in accurate estimates before you build." Everything else on the sheet is identical, stage for stage and deliverable for deliverable. The tiers are $4,950, $9,500, and $19,500.

One sentence changed and the price roughly doubled. A page that spends a line on what it isn't has already agreed to be the lesser thing. Taking the apology out was the pricing work.

Results

  • Three engagements on the shelf with published prices, from a $2,500 single-screen audit to a $19,500 eight-screen prototype. Fixed scope, fixed price, five days or less, and no proposal required to find out the number.
  • One page architecture across all three, bending only where the work does: Spark's fourth stage, Pulse's three deliverables, Surge's Figma file. A fourth product costs a page, not a redesign.
  • Two instrument panels running live on Spark and Pulse, built out of the direction I argued for over four others, on the grounds that showing the analysis beats claiming it.
